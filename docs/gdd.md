# SUMUD — Game Design Document v0.2

Sep 29, 2026 · @Gulrez Alam

## Vision

SUMUD (Arabic for steadfastness) is a 2D narrative adventure about one family on one street in Gaza City, told over ten days through a 12-year-old girl who flies kites. There is no combat. There is a perpetrator, called "they", shown as drones, gunboats, tanks, leaflets and soldiers behind lights, never as a character. The player's work is care: carry, feed, light, teach, bury, marry, and fly a kite. On 24 August 2026 Israel's defence minister declared that a kite launched from Gaza is treated as a drone, with or without explosives, and threatened strikes and evacuations ([Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2655710/middle-east), [Middle East Eye](https://www.middleeasteye.net/news/israel-warns-strikes-evacuations-over-kites-launched-palestinian-children-gaza)); the next day Gaza's own popular committees, coordinating with Hamas authorities, asked parents to stop their children flying kites, and parents began hiding and burning them ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/27/three-killed-in-strikes-on-gaza-as-israel-renews-threats-over-kite-flying)). That fact turns the ending from a metaphor into an act.

**The pitch in one line:** Inside meets This War of Mine, with the heart of Life is Strange, set in Gaza, with a score made only of human voices.

**The feeling we are chasing.** The quiet rooftop moment in Life is Strange where you just sit. The warmth of Thimbleweed Park, where every character has a joke. The dread and beauty of Limbo and Inside, where the world is a silhouette and light is precious. The loyalty between two children across a kite line in The Kite Runner. We want players to close the game and feel two things at once: grief, and the certainty that these people are not victims; they are tired, afraid, angry and funny, and they are still there, which is not a comfort but a fact the player has to sit with.

**Why this will sell, honestly.** Narrative was the top genre by games passing 1,000 Steam reviews in 2025, but the games closest to this one sit far lower: as of September 2026 Path Out has about 192 Steam reviews, Bury Me, My Love about 129 despite a BAFTA and Washington Post praise, 1979 Revolution: Black Friday about 1,022 after a 304,741 USD Kickstarter and four years of work, and Liyla and the Shadows of War, free and covered worldwide, could not fund its developer's next game for years. The base case is therefore 200 to 1,500 reviews, not "covered by every outlet". The two comparable small games about Palestine are Liyla (2016) and the coming Dreams on a Pillow (a 12-person team, 240,502 USD raised, 2027 target, per [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/dreams-on-a-pillow-raises-240-000-to-spotlight-two-decades-of-untold-palestinian-history-)), and neither is a narrative game about Gaza now; SUMUD arrives first and is pitched as the present-day companion to that 1948 story, never its competitor. What earns coverage beyond "a Gaza game" is the kite campaign, the street that refuses to stop being a street, and a score of human voices with no generative AI and no instruments, which also speaks to the Turkish, Indonesian, Malay and Urdu markets on the localisation list. The honest shape of what ships in 2027 is a two-hour game at 9.99 to 12.99 USD, not a five-hour one; the reasons are in Production plan.

## Design pillars

Every feature, scene and line of dialogue must serve at least one pillar and contradict none. If it fails this test it is cut.

1. **Steadfastness is a verb.** The player never fights. They carry, repair, share, teach, cook, wait, bury, marry and fly. Every mechanic is an act of care or persistence. Progress is measured in what is kept alive, not what is destroyed.
2. **They have no face, but they are there.** Characters say "they", "the army", "the occupation", "the planes", "the zanana". Israel is named plainly once in the fiction, on the title card, and again in the sources and on the store page. The occupation is shown as things: the drone hum that never stops, a gunboat and its searchlight on the horizon, a tank silhouette at the end of the street, leaflets falling before the evacuation, soldiers as shadows behind lights at the corridor checkpoint. Never a villain character, never a target. Defiance is played, not fought: Baba rows out under the gunboat, Teta holds the key at the checkpoint, Karim confronts and chooses, Layla flies the kite they have called a weapon. The family's answer to Karim's anger is the one Gaza gives, the stairwell school and the morning under the gunboat, never a lecture that violence is wrong.
3. **Joy is not optional.** Every day contains laughter, food, football, news and teasing, a song or a wedding. If a playtester says the game is only sad, the day is redesigned. Gaza's humour is the loudest thing in the room.
4. **One family, one street, one sea.** Five spaces: the beach, the street, the home and its roof, the UNRWA school courtyard, the harbour. Each has three states: before, siege, after. Small space, deep detail. Players know every named neighbour by the end of Day 3.
5. **Choices leave marks, not branches.** No good or bad endings. Choices change presence and light: who sits at the wedding, which windows are lit, which kites rise beside Sami's on Day 10, and what Teta recites. No choice decides who dies. Everything the player does is remembered in Layla's notebook; nothing is judged.
6. **Halal by design, and proud of it.** The score is human voices and the world. No instrument except the duff, and the duff only where the Sunnah places it, at the wedding. Quran is a full stop, never a background. No alcohol, no romance on screen, no mixed dancing, no women's dancing shown, no AI-generated Quran, voices or music. These rules are printed on the store page so the claim can be checked.

## Tone and ethics

This is a game about a real, ongoing catastrophe with real dead. The rules below exist so the game honours that and survives press, platform review and community scrutiny. Every rule was checked against sources this week; the corrections are noted where we were wrong.

- **Specific, not generic.** The game is set in Gaza City and says so. Places are real: the beach, the harbour, Al-Rashid coastal road, the UNRWA school. Food is Gazan, not generic Palestinian: sumaghiyyeh for the wedding batch (handed to neighbours at weddings and Eid), zibdiyit gambari for the fisherman, dagga with bread every day, qidra for the feast rice, knafeh ghazawiya (nuts and cinnamon, not cheese) on Day 1, per [Palestinian cuisine, Gaza dishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palestinian_cuisine) and [Sumaghiyyeh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumaghiyyeh). Our first draft's maqluba and musakhan were pan-Palestinian slips.
- **Gazan, not South Asian.** The founder's own frame is Indian, so the slips to watch are written down: Teta or Sitti as the readers choose, never Dadi; a Palestinian qari, not a Pakistani lecturer; sahja, not qawwali; a Palestinian nasheed, not an Urdu one; adults called by kunya, Abu Karim and Um Karim, not by first name; kites that fly, and no manja.
- **Fictional people, true situations.** No character is a real person. Every situation is drawn from documented testimony, and the Sources section at the end lists what each day is based on. The family is no longer called Haddad: a Hamas commander of that name was killed in Gaza City in May 2026 and hostile coverage would draw the line. The working name is the Awad family until the Palestinian readers choose one. We also avoid Bakr (the four boys killed on the beach in 2014 were a fishing family, our exact premise), Samouni, al-Dalu, Rajab, Doghmush and al-Bardaweel, each attached to a real tragedy or armed clan. Karim's football on the sand on Day 2 is played on the beach where the Bakr boys were killed, on purpose and in their memory, and the readers say whether it stays.
- **Violence is heard and felt. Death is shown as aftermath, never as impact.** A strike is one real, ugly, close bang, then silence, a ringing tone and dust. The dead are shown the way Gaza shows them: a small shape wrapped in white carried through the street, a hand-drawn poster with his name on the school wall, a kite that no longer flies. No blood, no body at the moment of death, no camera on a wound.
- **Children die in this story because they die in Gaza.** UNICEF reported at least 21,289 children killed between 7 October 2023 and 3 February 2026 ([UNICEF situation update](https://www.unicef.org/sop/reports/unicef-state-palestine-humanitarian-situation-update)), and about one child a day during the ceasefire since. Sami, 12, Layla's cousin and closest friend, dies on Day 7. The game never uses it as a twist, never films the impact, and lets Layla's own danger stay non-graphic. Target rating: PEGI 16, with 12 possible, and ESRB T, with a content warning written now. The death is never preventable by the player, never a mechanic, never a punishment.
- **"They", never "al-yahud".** Gazans colloquially say al-yahud for the army and the state. After its 2025 review of a Gaza documentary the BBC told staff to translate the word literally, as Jews, and that row shows why it would swallow the game's reception ([Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/diaspora/antisemitism/article-861296)). Characters say "they", "the army", "the occupation", "the planes", "the zanana". Israel is named once, plainly.
- **No slogans in dialogue.** Characters talk about bread, school, football, the wedding, the boat. The politics is in what happens to them.
- **Faith is lived, not preached.** The adhan marks the phases of every day. Characters pray, make dua, say "inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un" at the moment of loss and "hasbunallahu wa ni'mal wakeel" under the bombardment, say bismillah, alhamdulillah, inshallah and Allah yustur through the day with some left untranslated in the subtitles, and wash and bury their dead. Verses and hadith appear only in held, silent moments with their reference printed, each verified against quran.com and sunnah.com (see Scripture moments).
- **Sensitivity readers before any public build.** Two Palestinian readers, ideally from Gaza, review the script and the day designs; their notes are binding. We have none yet, so this is a launch gate, not a preference. They are paid, and credited as they wish. The routes are We Are Not Numbers, whose Gaza writers can be paid as readers, Palestinian Voices in Games, and Rasheed Abueideh, approached as a peer with something to offer, not as prior art. One named scholar reviews the audio rules and the wedding storyboard before the vertical slice.
- **Hope is earned.** The ending is not a rescue. The ending is that they are still there, and the player understands why that is a victory.

## Story

One family, the Awad household (working name), on a street of Gaza City near the sea, over ten days that begin with a kite festival and a wedding nine days away, and end with that wedding held in a school courtyard among the rubble. The through-line is a question Teta asks on Day 1 and the player answers on Day 10: what does it mean to stay? The ten-day structure stands. The first draft's 25 to 35 minutes a day do not: four tentpole days of 15 to 20 minutes and six short days of 5 to 10 minutes, about two hours in all, which is what one engineer can build well by the end of December.

**Setting.** The beach, the street with its bakery and clinic, the home and its roof, the UNRWA school, the harbour: five spaces, each in three states (before, siege, after), re-dressed by damage and light. The year is never stated; the conditions are those of 2023 to 2026, compressed into ten days: the before days carry the blockade's grid hours and a school that is still a school, the siege days the war, and the sumud days the ceasefire, whose weddings, football on the ruins and kites are all in the Sources. The sea has no fixed limit any more: it was 20 nautical miles under Oslo, swung between 3 and 15 with 14 changes in 2019 alone, and has been closed outright since January 2025, with fishermen shot even after the October 2025 ceasefire ([OCHA](https://www.ochaopt.org/content/gaza-s-fisheries-record-expansion-fishing-limit-and-relative-increase-fish-catch-shooting), [UN Protection Cluster](https://www.un.org/unispal/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/OPT-Protection-Cluster_Building-to-Starvation-Systematic-Attacks-on-Fishing-in-Gaza.pdf)). In the game the limit is wherever they say it is today.

**Cast.** Each family member is playable on specific days and has one physical verb and one social verb. Eight named neighbours, not twenty; they remember everything.

**Choices that leave marks.** Who gets the last generator battery (the clinic or the school). Whether Karim puts his name down for a scholarship abroad, or stays. Which neighbour you carry on Day 7 when you can carry one. Whether Layla gives her own kite away on Day 9. None are graded. Each changes who sits at the wedding, which windows are lit, which kites rise beside Sami's, and what Teta recites. None decides who dies: the neighbour you do not carry on Day 7 reaches the school later, by another door, and remembers it.

**Sami.** He is the game's second child and its one death. He has a beat on every day from 1 to 6: the contest and the promise, football, holding the candle in the bakery, the jerrycan race, the message line between the two roofs, watching for Baba's boat. He dies off-screen on Day 7 in the strike behind the family as they walk, and Layla learns it the way children in Gaza learn it: someone says his name. Day 8 is his janazah, and the grief is tears, not wailing, as Sahih al-Bukhari 1284 shows it; the street calls him shahid, and nobody says he passed away. The 'aza customs, whether names are written on children's arms, the hand-drawn poster and whether a martyr's funeral may rhyme with the wedding are the readers' to settle before they are built. Day 9 the street rebuilds his kite, one part from each family: thread from the dress, paper from Mama's books, spars from Baba's boat, paste from Abu Ahmad's flour, string from Karim's wire. Day 10 Layla keeps the promise and flies it. The death is caused by no choice the player made, cannot be prevented, is never a fail state, and is never filmed. The cases this is modelled on are in the Sources: the eight-year-old killed when shelling hit the shelter beside a wedding tent in Gaza City in December 2025, and a four-year-old reported by a Gaza writer to have been killed after flying a kite in August 2026 (single-sourced, so it is a tone reference, not a claim the game makes).

**Ending.** The wedding happens. The family walks to the beach at dusk. They have been told a kite is a drone. Layla flies Sami's kite, and every kite the player helped build or save rises with it, one per remembered act of care, and the kites the player designed. Teta says the line from Day 1. They are still there. Credits over the sea. An end card gives the UNICEF figure with its date.

**Prologue and title card.** The game opens on the sea, with a kite, before any tension, and the title card appears only after the first strike on Day 3. Players should have fallen in love with the street before it is threatened.

**What we take from The Kite Runner, and what we do not.** We take the loyalty between two children across a kite line, kite running as a way to move through a city, and a promise kept for a friend who is gone. We do not take kite fighting with glass string, which is South Asian and Afghan and does not exist in Gaza (our first draft imported it), nor the betrayal plot, nor a guilt and redemption arc for Layla, nor escape to the West as Karim's salvation: her story is care, and his choice is not a rescue.

## Core mechanics

The game is a side-scrolling adventure with physics puzzles, built on five systems that feed one loop: decide what to carry, cross the street, help someone, bring light home, sit on the roof, write the notebook. Nothing here needs combat, enemies or pathfinding, which keeps the build within reach of one engineer. Carry and the kite already run in the beach greybox and pass a headless bot test; light and the street's memory are Week 1 and Week 2 work.

The loop repeats ten times with the same six steps; what changes is how much light and bread there is, and who is still on the street.

**1. Carry (the spine).** The player has two hands and a bag of three small slots (matches, the key, a phone, bread, a note, the notebook). Everything that matters is carried: a 20-litre jerrycan, a car battery, a kite, a radio, a child, a wounded neighbour, the grandmother's key. Weight changes movement, and the rules are already in the build: a light item leaves the body free; a heavy one-hand item slows the run to six tenths and removes the jump; a two-handed load (a person, a full drum, a beam) slows it to under half, removes the jump and the crawl, and cannot open a door, so the route itself has to change. Layla cannot lift an adult; Baba can; Teta carries only the bag; Karim carries one-hand loads at full speed. Choosing the load is choosing the day's plan. On Day 7 the corridor loudhailer orders "hands up" while the player's hands are full, and choosing who to carry is the game's hardest decision, made with the hands, not a menu. A checkpoint sits at every doorway and every set-down, so a wrong choice costs seconds.

**2. The kite (Layla's signature verb), and the sky that opens and closes.** Wind is a real, visible field in every outdoor level, shown by dust, plastic bags and washing lines. Layla launches the kite when a gust stirs the dust at her feet, pays out string and steers it. It scouts (the camera follows it over walls, which also gives us cinematics for free), carries light things across gaps and up to roofs (a note, matches, a key), tugs hooks, catches a falling leaflet on Day 7, and signals neighbours by colour. When a kite falls, Layla runs it down through the street: kite running is the traversal set piece of Day 1, and it returns on Day 9 when the rebuilt kite's first flight falls. There is no kite fighting; Gaza's kite culture is making and flying, and the festival is a record attempt with a whistle and a 30-second count. From Day 2 the drone is in the sky as a sound with an intensity from 0 to 1 on a schedule for each day. Above a threshold the sky is closed: Layla will not launch, neighbours dim their own windows, people go indoors, the hum is everywhere. Below it, the sky opens. The player reads the sky like a tide and plans around it, and every flight on a siege day is a small defiance: scouting the collapsed bakery from above on Day 3, spotting which end of the street the water truck stopped at on Day 4, the message line on Day 5, signalling the boat on Day 6, reading the route over the rubble on Day 7. The kite is lost on Day 7 and returned, as Sami's kite, on Day 9.

**3. Light and power.** Electricity arrives on a schedule that shrinks each day, then stops: half a day on Days 1 and 2, nothing from Day 3, two generator hours a night in the shelter, phone torches as the default light. Batteries, fuel and Abu Fadi's solar panels are the scarce currency. Lamps, candles and batteries are carry objects; every home has a lamp hook by its window; a lit lamp on a hook lights the window; one fuel is one night. The phone light is personal, infinite and dim, so no player is ever stuck in the dark, but some things can only be read by lamp light. Darkness is not lethal but it is impassable without a flame. Each night the player decides which rooms and which neighbours get power by carrying light to them. The street at night, with its lit and dark windows, is the game's only status display. There is no morale bar. You look up and see who has light.

**4. The street (community memory).** Eight named neighbours with needs that change daily: bread, water, medicine, a charge, company, a game of marbles. There is no quest log, and needs are never text: an open door means come in, a dark window means they need light, chalk marks on the wall are the race scores, a plate left outside means they cooked for you. Helping is noticed and comes back later without being announced, from a small table of act, day done, and payoff: the family you carried water to on Day 4 is the one who saves a seat at the wedding; the bread you brought the twins on Day 3 is why their family brings a part for Sami's kite on Day 9. One playthrough can help about six in ten of the street, so the empty chairs at the wedding are the reason to play again. Every act is written in Layla's notebook, which is the journal, the save summary and the list of remembered acts that becomes kites in the final sky.

**5. Character switching.** At the home hub and at fixed points the player switches between whoever is available that day, as in Thimbleweed Park. Puzzles are designed around combinations: Layla crawls into the collapsed bakery to reach the flour, Baba lifts the beam, Teta persuades the owner to share the oven. No character is a worse version of another.

**Time.** Days advance by what the player does, not by a clock. The phases are the prayer times, fajr, dhuhr, asr, maghrib and isha, and the adhan marks each turn, so the mosque is present in every day without a line of dialogue. There is no timer stress, but the sun visibly moves and some things exist only at night.

**Puzzles.** Inside-style physical puzzles built kinematic-first from a kit of six pieces (plank, cart, rubble pile, jerrycan, beam, hook), one or two per day, with a reset input always available and a checkpoint seconds back. No rope or water simulation: ropes are drawn lines with constraints, and the boat is a scripted scene. No inventory combining, no pixel hunting. A debug overlay exposes gravity, jump, carry factors, kite drag and wind strength so the founder can tune the feel in play and paste the numbers back.

**Danger, and the surprise.** Jets and shells whistle before they land; the drone gives no warning. That documented asymmetry is the game's surprise: sometimes the birds leave the roofs and you find cover, and sometimes nothing warns you. A telegraphed strike runs on a fixed clock: the hum rises and the birds lift, then the rumble, then the flash, about twelve seconds in all for the first strike on Day 3 and four for the ones after; cover is any interior or doorway. Failure is a white screen of dust and a return to the last doorway, seconds back. No death animation, ever, for a character the player controls, and no timed input ever decides whether anyone lives: missing cover costs a white-out and a few seconds, never a death.

**Intensity, rationed.** The game is calm, so intensity is spent where it is true, at most one beat a day (Day 3 has two, one by day and one by night), none on Day 7 or Day 10, every one diegetic (hands, feet, breath, a cord, a cane), never a music-driven obstacle course, and never deciding whether anyone lives. The research on where timed moments work in narrative games (Inside, Until Dawn, Life is Strange, Valiant Hearts, Florence) and the accessibility guidelines behind Steam's "playable at your own pace" tag are in research/08; the rules are: teach before you test, failure costs seconds, one input per moment (press or hold, never mash, never two buttons), and timing never gates reading. The beats: Day 3, the birds leave and you have about twelve seconds to get down the stairs; Day 3 night, hold to cup the candle flame when a draft is telegraphed; Day 4, the jerrycan race, a real footrace; Day 5, the hum, where the player must release every input and let the kite hang still until the drone passes; Day 6, hold and release to haul the net on the swell, then one wide window to turn home under the searchlight; Day 8, pull the starter cord on Karim's count; Day 9, stamp on Teta's cane taps in the dabke line. A single "own pace" option pauses every timer until input and adds per-moment auto-complete, so the game can honestly carry Steam's accessibility tags. The bigger surprises are removals: Day 3 takes the light, Day 7 takes the kite and a friend and has no timed input at all, only weight. In-engine cinematics with letterbox, built from the kite camera, an animation player and the day's lighting, never taking control away for more than the title card's three seconds: the count to thirty, the first strike, the corridor, the janazah, the zaffa, the sky.

**Quiet moments.** Sit spots on the roof, the beach and the stairwell. Sitting starts the voices and a slow conversation, and it is where dua, a verse or a hadith may appear, held, with everything else silent. Optional, unrewarded, and the thing people will remember. One candidate line for Teta on the roof is that Imam al-Shafi'i was born on this sand, which most classical biographies say; the readers confirm it before it is written, and nothing about Gaza's own history goes into her mouth unchecked. Joy is played, not watched: football against two kids, the jerrycan race, marbles with the old fisherman, and a street cat that follows whoever carries fish and adopts the family.

**Small things that make it lovable.** The 1948 key can be tried in every door in the game; nothing opens, Teta has a different line each time, and on Day 10 she tries it on the school gate. Layla writes bismillah at the top of every notebook page, set clean, untouched by the grain and ink passes. On Day 8 a page appears in the notebook in Sami's handwriting, the note he sent down the line on Day 5. On Day 10 the walk to the beach passes the dedicated kites, each with its name, before the flight. When the game is finished, the title screen shows your own Day 10 sky, the notebook exports day by day, the wedding photo (the men's courtyard in silhouette) is composed from who you helped and can be saved, and a day select carries your notebook, so the empty chairs are visible and the street can be played again.

**Controls.** Keyboard and every major controller, fully remappable, and a virtual joystick with touch buttons for the later phone build. Move, jump, crawl, interact, grab and release, kite, switch character, notebook. Eight inputs, learnable on Day 1 without a tutorial screen.

## Art direction: Shadow and Thread, with colour in the light

The look is layered silhouettes lit by scarce, warm light, Palestinian embroidery as the language of the interface, and kites as the purest colour in the world. It is chosen because it reads as a deliberate, premium style and because every element can be generated from code and shaders with no painter and no 3D.

**The honest answer on more colour: yes, but as light, never as paint.** We checked how the reference games get their colour. Limbo is monochrome with grain. Inside is near-monochrome with one or two accents, and its beauty is volumetric light and dithering ([Playdead's GDC talk](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023002/Low-Complexity-High-Fidelity-INSIDE)). Badland stacks a blurred, coloured, hazy background under a pure-black gameplay layer, with fog and glow layers between ([Juhana Myllys](https://www.jmyllys.com/badland)). Kentucky Route Zero is flat colour that works because it is lit like a stage: darkness and one or two coloured lights ([Tamas Kemenczy](https://usesthis.com/interviews/tamas.kemenczy/)). Gris, Journey and Night in the Woods get their colour from painters, which we do not have. So the premium result for a code-only pipeline is Badland's layer model with Inside's accent rule: colour lives in the sky, the sea, the haze, the light spill and a few accent objects (kites, tatreez red, bread crust, fire, the wedding dress), and the silhouettes stay near-black to deep indigo. Flat coded colour reads as cheap exactly when it is a large untextured fill, a banded gradient, a saturated mid-tone, several competing hues, or clean identical shapes; we avoid all five. The truth is that more colour than Limbo is right for this game, and colour everywhere would destroy both the look and the meaning of the kites.

**Colour is a budget the days spend.** One per-day value drives every shader. Days 1 and 2 are the most coloured: gold dawn, blue sea, rose dusk, kites at full saturation. Day 3's first strike collapses the world to grey plus fire orange. Days 4 to 7 stay desaturated, with colour only where care happens: the oven glow, the candle, the water in the jerrycan; the walk to the school on Day 7 is nearly monochrome. Days 8 to 10 restore hue in step with the remembered acts (the generator light, the henna, the dress), and the Day 10 sky of kites is the most saturated frame of the game. The per-day palette is one JSON file the founder can tune.

**World.** Five to seven parallax layers of silhouetted buildings, minarets, water tanks, washing lines and rubble, generated from polygons with noise-roughened edges so no two buildings match. Volumetric light shafts, drifting dust, smoke, sea haze and rain are particle and shader effects. Windows are small lights that also serve as the community display at night. The lighting stack is Godot's own: a CanvasModulate for the ambient base, a directional light for sun and moon, point lights with grey-scale textures for windows, candles and the oven, occluders generated with the silhouettes, and 2D HDR glow for kites, fire and the final sky. Three giveaways of cheap coded colour are killed in the prototype: banding (a blue-noise dither pass), staircase edges (Forward+ with 2D multisampling), and sameness (seeded jitter on every generated shape, plus paper grain, ink bleed and vignette so the image reads as printed, not rendered).

**Characters.** Silhouettes with one readable trait each. Layla wears a white headscarf outdoors and at school, as Gaza school custom expects of girls from about her age (a detail for the readers to confirm), and her trait is the kite string and the kite itself; her hair shows only at home among family. Adult women wear hijab and thobe or abaya outside the home. Teta's cane and long thobe, Baba's cap and shoulders, Rania's bag of books, Karim's ball. Two small warm eye-lights make faces readable. Men's dabke is shown in silhouette; women's dancing is heard, never shown. Animation is pose-tween: eight to ten code-generated poses per character, blended, with lean and speed driven by the carry rules; the body genuinely leans under a jerrycan. Inverse kinematics, if used at all, is a few lines of GDScript for feet and hands, because Godot 4.7 still marks its IK modifiers experimental.

**Thread.** Menus, chapter cards, the notebook and the wedding dress use tatreez, the Palestinian cross-stitch, inscribed by UNESCO in 2021. It is grid-based and therefore procedural: the generator already produces bands of cypress, star, chevron, lattice and moon motifs, and the chapter card stitches itself in while the day loads. Red on black and cream sets the interface palette. Arabic text never bends into a kite or a motif; Quran text is shown clean and legible or not at all.

**Camera and format.** Side view with depth, 16:9 at 1920 by 1080, scaling to 4K, letterboxed for cinematic beats, Steam Deck legibility checked at 1280 by 800.

**Production of assets.** The world (skies, sea, skyline, buildings, the grade, tatreez) stays generated by code, versioned and regenerated on demand. People are made by people: characters by rotoscoped performance or an artist's cut-out rig, chosen by an animation test in the lock; hero props drawn by hand; voices, foley and ambience recorded. From 1 October 2026 generative image models are used for characters' frames, props and reference, under the limits in `docs/production/lock.md` F-09 (never Quran, never music, nothing from real Gazans' footage or likeness, every asset reviewed and listed in `docs/rights.md`, disclosed on Steam); generated sound waits on a route the project can reach (`docs/art/direction.md`, `docs/audio/direction.md`).

## Audio: a score made only of voices

The founder's rule is absolute halal. Under the relied-upon position of the four schools every instrument is excluded except the duff, and the duff only at weddings and Eid ([islamqa 20406](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/20406), [islamweb 339946](https://www.islamweb.net/en/fatwa/339946/ruling-on-percussions-other-than-duff)); a synthesised pad that sounds like a string or a reed carries the same ruling, because what counts is what reaches the ears ([islamqa 229732](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/229732)). So the oud, qanun, ney, piano and strings of the first draft are gone, and the score is human voice and the world. This is an identity, not a loss: the film ROSE (Berlinale 2026) has an entirely a cappella score that critics called holy, Maher Zain's Thank You Allah sold hundreds of thousands and was reissued as a vocals-only edition, and Ahmed Bukhatir has sung without instruments for his whole career. We will market it as a score made only of voices, the way Limbo's monochrome was an identity.

**The score.** One human voice, the friend's, recorded on a phone in a quiet room: a cappella nasheed lines in maqam (the maqamat themselves are permitted), humming, held vowels on a few pitches that code layers into pads, self-harmonies. Nothing that imitates an instrument, no beatboxing that imitates drums. The dabke is carried by feet, voices and zaghareet; clapping is disputed and used sparingly. A main theme recurs across the ten days in different vocal arrangements; the mix ducks and swells with day, night, threat and rest. Nasheed may be faded and interrupted freely; that rule does not apply to Quran.

**Teta's songs.** A recorded adult woman singing to a mixed audience is the highest halal risk in the plan under both strict and permissive views ([islamqa 91142](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/91142)). So the sung melodies are carried by Layla, a child, or Teta speaks her lullaby as poetry over a vocal drone, and the women's henna singing on Day 9 is heard through a wall from the men's side, with the zaghareet in the clear; the duff is kept for the wedding itself unless the scholar allows it at the henna. Wedding and rooftop lyrics stay about home, sea, bread and family, and never claim the unseen, the rule the Prophet applied to the girls singing at a wedding (Sahih al-Bukhari 5147).

**Teta at bedtime.** On some nights the night scene ends with Teta reciting Ayat al-Kursi and the three Quls over the children. It is Quran, so it keeps the full-stop rules below: a held moment, every bus silent, the text clean on screen with its reference, the audio the recorded reciter's or none, never under the drone and never under play. The four references join Scripture moments once they have passed the same check.

**The adhan is the clock.** The five phases of each day turn with the call to prayer, recorded by a human (the friend or a local muezzin), which is halal, free, and gives the mosque a presence in every day.

**The zanana.** The surveillance drone's buzz is the ambient bed of every siege day, day and night, exactly as Gazans describe it ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/opinions/2025/1/21/i-dream-of-a-quiet-drone-free-gaza), [We Are Not Numbers](https://wearenotnumbers.org/drones-above-gaza/)). It is absent on Day 1, arrives as the first faint hum on Day 2, and is gone on the final beach, so its absence lands.

**Strikes: decided.** One real, close, sub-heavy impact per strike day (Days 3 and 7), then silence, a ringing tone and dust, with everything else ducked. Jets and shells whistle first; the drone does not. Everything else is distant thuds in the bed. Explosion, artillery and vehicle sounds are explicitly permissible in the rulings we read, with one condition we keep: no such sound ever plays under recitation. The loud strike gets its own volume slider beside the rumble bus, and the photosensitivity mode removes its flash.

**Quran is a full stop.** Every scholar we read agrees that Quran must not run under distraction or games ([islamqa 88728](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/88728), [islamweb 86274](https://www.islamweb.net/en/fatwa/86274/hear-quran-while-playing)), must not have music or effects under it ([islamqa 271405](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/271405)), and must not be cut mid-verse ([islamweb 267796](https://www.islamweb.net/en/fatwa/267796/waiting-until-the-end-of-a-chapter-or-verse-to-switch-off-the-quran)). So a verse or hadith appears only in a paused, silent listen state: gameplay stops, the camera holds on a non-human subject (sea, sky, hands, bread, the dedicated kites), every bus is muted, one complete ayah or short passage plays, the Arabic in clean Uthmani script with a translation and the reference are on screen, and skip works only at an ayah boundary. Verses are chosen to match what is literally on screen (the sea under the sea verse, bread under the feeding verse) and never the unseen over a worldly image ([islamqa 175174](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/175174)). No verse on a fail screen, in a comic beat, or as set dressing on walls. Hadith are Sahih or Hasan only, with collection and number. The list is in Scripture moments.

**Recitation audio: the truth about famous reciters.** No famous reciter's recording can ship. Mishary Alafasy's recordings belong to Alafasy Berhad, all rights reserved; quran.com and quranicaudio.com permit personal, non-commercial use only; everyayah.com's licence link is dead and a mirror states the recitation licence is unknown ([quran.com terms](https://quran.com/en/terms-and-conditions), [quranicaudio](https://quranicaudio.com/about)). Our options, in order: a local hafiz or the friend records the fourteen Quran passages in Scripture moments and the four passages of Teta's bedtime under a written licence (a Palestinian qari would be the honest and marketable choice); written permission from a reciter or rights-holder, allowing four to eight weeks; or text on screen with no audio, which is always safe. AI recitation is out entirely: islamqa permits it only with the voice owner's permission, Al-Azhar condemned AI Quran clips in 2024, and ElevenLabs forbids cloning anyone but yourself.

**Dr Israr Ahmed and other scholars.** His lectures are all rights reserved by Tanzeem-e-Islami with no published reuse policy (permission via markaz@tanzeem.org), and his official YouTube channel was terminated in April 2022 under YouTube's hate-speech policy ([Kashmir Life](https://kashmirlife.net/youtube-terminates-preacher-dr-israr-ahmads-official-channel-289861/)), which hostile press would attach to the game. Recommendation: a short attributed quotation from his published tafsir on a text card, with permission, or nothing at launch. Never a synthesised scholar's voice; the community already treats deepfaked scholars as a crisis.

**AI tools: the truth.** ElevenLabs paid plans (from 6 USD) carry a commercial licence for speech, but Arabic has no dialect control, so a Gazan voice is not a feature; cloning is your own voice only; and Eleven Music's self-serve licence excludes "Studio Games" ([Eleven Music terms](https://elevenlabs.io/eleven-music-model-specific-terms)). Google Flow is a video tool; the music product is Flow Music on Lyria, with commercial rights only through a paid Google AI membership. Suno Pro can export vocal stems; Udio can no longer export at all. Steam requires disclosure on the store page of any AI content that ships in the game or its marketing, and the community has punished visible AI in The Alters (from the studio behind This War of Mine), Arc Raiders, Party Animals and Vapor World. Decision: no AI-generated audio ships. If a temporary AI voice is ever used to block out a scene, it stays out of the repository and is replaced before any build leaves the machine. The store page will say "no generative AI was used to make this game", which for this audience is a selling point.

**Ambience.** Field recordings only from provenance we can show: the CC0 Gaza pack recorded in the Gaza Strip in 2016 ([Freesound](https://freesound.org/people/kyles/sounds/407336/)), a Palestinian recordist's West Bank street set ([sounds\_from\_palestine](https://freesound.org/people/sounds_from_palestine/)), Sonniss and Kenney libraries for impacts and debris. Wind, sea, dust and rumble are synthesised so they follow the wind field. We reject anything ripped from a livestream and any real grief clip, whatever its licence tag. Every recordist is credited.

**Voice acting.** None at launch; dialogue is subtitled with Arabic on screen and English below. Full Arabic voice acting, by humans, is a post-launch goal.

**Rights ledger and mix.** A file in the repository lists every audio asset, its source, licence, date and who approved it, which is also the answer to Steam's content survey. Buses: voices, ambience, effects, rumble, strike.

## Go-to-market

Steam first on Windows and Linux, which makes Steam Deck nearly free; a Day 1 demo in the February 2027 Next Fest; launch in March 2027 at 9.99 to 12.99 USD for a two-hour game; macOS when a Mac tester exists; Android before iOS.

**Platforms and price.** Verified Steam prices today: Inside 24.99 USD, Gris 14.99, Valiant Hearts 14.99, cut to 3.74 in a 75 percent weekend deal. A first game of about two hours from a new studio sits at 9.99 to 12.99, with a launch discount and no microtransactions of any kind. Mobile is deliberately later: Apple rejected Liyla and the Shadows of War from the App Store's Games category on 18 May 2016, reversed by 20 May after outcry, and the app was live in the Games category by 22 May ([TouchArcade](https://toucharcade.com/2016/05/18/apple-says-liyla-and-the-shadows-of-war-is-not-a-game/)), so Android comes first when the time comes. The current rules make the caution stronger: Apple's guideline 1.1.2 bars enemies that solely target a real government or other real entity and 1.1.7 bars content that seeks to profit from current violent conflicts, and Google Play's sensitive-events policy bans appearing to profit from one with no discernible benefit to the victims, with an exception for educational, documentary or artistic content. The charity share and the Sources tab are what make that exception arguable, so both stay on the store page from day one.

**The kites.** At launch, an in-game kite designer: the kites you design rise in your own Day 10 sky, plus a curated batch from testers and the community baked in. The designer is a pattern generator, never free drawing, so nothing offensive can be drawn into a build. The public web page where anyone dedicates a kite that flies in everyone's ending follows after launch, because it needs a moderation queue and privacy handling, which is a month of work and a liability on launch day. When it comes: a dedication is a first name and a short phrase from an allow-list in English and Arabic; a kite for a real killed child is accepted only from that child's family or from an organisation that consents, and no name is scraped from a list; a human sees every kite before it is packed; the set is frozen per build; the rules and a takedown route are published on the page; and the moderation hours are budgeted, because one wrong kite ends up in every stream and screenshot. The sky is a tribute, not a database.

**Community and testers.** A Discord from the first public build; weekly posts showing the generators at work (a street growing, a card stitching itself); five testers by Week 4 and eight by Week 11, screen-recorded, unhelped, asked one question afterwards: what moment stuck. Narrative-game streamers get the demo a week early. If the reviews are bombed, the store text stays about the street and not the state, Valve's off-topic review system is left on, one calm paragraph written now goes on the community hub, and anomalies are reported through Steamworks, never argued in threads.

**Reaching people with no following.** The store page comes first: the Steam Direct fee starts a 30-day wait, a Coming Soon page must be visible for two weeks and takes 3 to 5 business days to review, so the fee is paid this week and the page goes live in Week 4 with the hero shots. The health check is 150 wishlists in the first two weeks; the realistic launch target is 1,000 to 3,000, not the 7,000 that Popular Upcoming has needed. The channels that have reached this exact audience are the itch.io charity bundles (the 2021 Palestinian Aid bundle raised 902,365 USD with Liyla as its anchor, Games for Gaza 365,554 USD in 2023, the Palestinian Relief Bundle 578,573 USD in 2024), so the demo, the vocal score and the kite patterns go into the next one; LaunchGood, which funded Dreams on a Pillow with 240,502 USD from more than 3,300 backers after other platforms refused it and fits the halal positioning if the singer, the readers or the moderation need money; a Steam Playtest, which needs no demo and leaves wishlists untouched, for the stranger tests already in the plan; direct emails with a demo key to the outlets that covered Dreams on a Pillow (The Guardian, Polygon, Al Jazeera, PC Gamer, Eurogamer, GamesIndustry.biz, Kotaku, The National, Hyphen) and to narrative streamers; and every non-Valve Steam festival. Devlogs and follower-building are skipped, because the data says they are time sinks. A one-page teacher's guide built from the Sources gives outlets a second story, as This War of Mine's place on Poland's school reading list did.

**Charity.** The founder's answer was "yes, 100%", which can mean an emphatic yes or all of the revenue. All of the revenue does not work: Steam keeps 30 percent; the United States withholds 15 percent of the rest from an Indian payee under the treaty; what arrives is taxable business income in India with no Section 80G deduction for a foreign charity; and sending it abroad under the liberalised remittance scheme draws 20 percent tax collected at source above 10 lakh rupees, reclaimable later. A 100 percent pledge therefore means paying tax out of pocket on money given away. Saying on a store page that purchases benefit a charity also makes the seller a commercial participator in the UK and a commercial co-venturer in seven US states, which needs a written agreement with the charity and a statement naming it and the amount. Recommendation: one sentence in the pattern that has sat on Steam since 2015 for This War of Mine's War Child DLC, "X percent of the developer's net receipts from Valve, after Steam's share, withholding, refunds and chargebacks, goes to [named charity]"; the charity named now and its written consent in hand before the page goes live, with Medical Aid for Palestinians (UK charity 1045315) and the Palestine Children's Relief Fund (US 501(c)(3)) as the candidates; a quarterly post showing the Valve statement and the charity's receipt instead of an audit the project cannot pay for; and, if the founder wants the "100%" headline, a launch-week pledge of all proceeds on top of the standing share and a supporter DLC in three price tiers with identical content, both proven by 11 bit. One hour with an Indian chartered accountant before the store page is submitted.

**Localisation.** English and Arabic at launch, Urdu and Hindi within a month, then French, Spanish, German, Turkish, Indonesian and Malay.

**Accessibility.** Remappable inputs; at most one timed beat a day (two on Day 3), all diegetic, with a hold-or-press choice and a single "own pace" option that pauses every timer until input and auto-completes on request, so the game honestly carries Steam's "playable without quick time events" and "playable at your own pace" tags; subtitle size and background options and a caption for every audio telegraph (birds, rumble, the hum, the loudhailer); separate sliders for rumble and the loud strike; a screen-shake toggle; a photosensitivity mode that removes the flash from strikes; kites distinguished by pattern as well as colour. Launch requirements, not stretch goals.

**Rating and content warning.** Neither PEGI nor ESRB has a child-death descriptor; what is rated is the act on screen and its realism. Valiant Hearts executes its protagonist on screen and is PEGI 12; Life is Strange shows a suicide jump and is PEGI 16; Inside's stylised boy is PEGI 18 because he is sliced and mauled in view. With Sami's death shown only as aftermath, no blood, no injury detail, no one shown harming a child and no strong language, the truthful target is PEGI 16 (12 is possible) and ESRB T. What would push it to 18 or M: any on-screen violence against a child with visible injury, a soldier or parent shown harming a child, gore in any style, or a timed input that could have saved him. The content warning is written now and shown on the store page and at first launch, and the founder fills the IARC questionnaire against Day 7 as actually built.

**No generative AI.** Steam requires disclosure of any AI-generated content that ships in the game or its marketing ([Steamworks content survey](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/contentsurvey)); AI coding assistants are exempt. Nothing generated ships, the rights ledger proves it, and the store page says so, which for this audience is a selling point.

**Press angle.** Not a war game. A game about a street that refuses to stop being a street, with a score made only of voices, whose ending flies the kites that have been declared weapons. Every preview build opens on the kite festival so the first screenshot anyone takes is joy. The end card carries a dated, attributed figure and is refreshed on launch day. The pitch says "made with, not for": the Palestinian readers, singers and consultants are credited above the fold on the store page and in the press kit, because coverage of Liyla and Dreams on a Pillow was anchored in their developer being Palestinian, and the first question to an Indian founder will be "why you".

## Decisions for the founder

Your answers of 29 September are locked: Limbo as the base, Godot, the cast, ten days, English and Arabic, player kites, the title SUMUD, one death and Karim's choice, a real explosion sound, no combat, "they". The research raised the decisions below. Each has my recommendation, so "go with your picks" is a complete reply.

1. **Sami's death and the rating.** Sami dies off-screen on Day 7 and is shown as aftermath; the game targets PEGI 16. Recommendation: yes.
2. **The family name.** Haddad is retired. Awad is the placeholder until the Palestinian readers choose. Recommendation: let the readers choose.
3. **The halal lens and the duff.** Pick one school's lens for the studio; Hanafi is natural for you and its answers on percussion are the most permissive of the mainstream. Under it, a duff on the women's side of the wedding is safe. Men clapping in the dabke is disputed; feet and voices are not. Recommendation: Hanafi lens, duff on the women's side, foot-stamps and voices for the men.
4. **Teta's songs.** Sung by Layla, or spoken by Teta as poetry over a vocal drone. Recommendation: Layla sings the lullaby; Teta speaks the wedding blessing.
5. **Charity.** What "100%" means, the share, and the organisation. Recommendation: a fixed share of net receipts from Valve in the War Child wording, Medical Aid for Palestinians or the Palestine Children's Relief Fund named on the store page with its written consent, a quarterly post of the Valve statement and the charity's receipt, and a launch-week 100 percent pledge if you want that headline.
6. **The calendar.** Content complete by 28 December, launch in March 2027 after Next Fest, instead of launching on 31 December. Recommendation: accept; the December date forfeits Next Fest and lands in the Winter Sale. Superseded on 30 September 2026: the founder agreed to move the date for quality; the launch is set at the Day 3 slice gate, not earlier than late 2027, with Next Fest two to four months before.
7. **The ending.** Layla flies a kite after they have declared kites drones, with the whole street, at the sea. It is the truest ending available and also the most exposed. Recommendation: yes, with Teta's line acknowledging the risk.
8. **Who records the ayat.** A local hafiz, the friend, or a Palestinian qari, under a written licence; no famous reciter can be used. Recommendation: a local hafiz for the prototype, a Palestinian qari for launch if one can be found.
9. **Dr Israr Ahmed.** A short attributed text quotation with permission from Tanzeem-e-Islami, or nothing at launch; never his voice. Recommendation: nothing at launch.
10. **Downloads.** Amiri and Noto Naskh Arabic (OFL, about 1.1 MB) were fetched into the repository for the prototype; GodotSteam comes later. Recommendation: keep them, and say so if you want them out.
11. **Steamworks.** Pay the 100 USD Steam Direct fee and start onboarding this week: identity, PAN, the tax interview. The fee starts Steam's 30-day clock, and onboarding can take one to three weeks. Recommendation: this week.
12. **Readers and testers.** Two Palestinian readers by Week 6 and five testers by Week 4; the game does not ship without the readers. Recommendation: start outreach today; I will draft the messages.
13. **Dhul Hijjah.** One critique proposes making the ten days the first ten of Dhul Hijjah, so the wedding falls on Eid al-Adha and the street chants the takbirat. It would fix a calendar the fiction keeps open, put a janazah and a wedding inside the Eid days, and add a religious frame the readers have not checked. Recommendation: no; if you want the takbirat, one Eid memory in Teta's stories is enough.
14. **Whose wedding.** The document never says who marries. Recommendation: the bride is Sami's older sister, Layla's cousin, so the wedding is the family's own, Day 8's question is whether to hold it two days after his janazah, and his father gives the blessing; the groom is a young man of the street. The readers check the custom; you decide the story.
