# Characters

Status: **PROPOSED for the story lock** (see `docs/production/lock.md`, section B and C). The design document's cast (`docs/gdd.md`, Story, Cast) is the base and is not changed here; this file deepens it and adds what the beat sheet needs: relations, wants, arcs, voices, and how each person moves, which is what a performer or an animator needs.

Every name is a working name until the Palestinian readers confirm it or choose another, and the family name is still the placeholder Awad. Every Arabic word or phrase in this file is a guess at Gazan usage for the readers to correct. Nothing here is a real person.

## Rules that apply to every character

- Adults are called by kunya in the street (Abu Karim, Um Karim), by relation in the family (Baba, Mama, Teta), never by first name by children.
- No romance on screen. The bride and groom are never shown together; the bride appears only in women's spaces.
- Women wear hijab and a long dress, jilbab or thobe outside the home and in front of men who are not family. At home among family, Layla's hair shows; she covers when a neighbour knocks.
- Nobody uses the colloquial word for "the Jews" (the design document, Tone and ethics). People say "they", "the army", "the occupation", "the planes", "the zanana".
- Nobody makes a speech. Politics is in what happens to them, never in what they say to the camera.
- Everyone is allowed to be tired, afraid, angry and funny. Nobody is a saint, and nobody is a symbol.

## The family: the Awad house (working name)

### Layla, 12. The protagonist

**Who she is.** Fast, funny, and she notices everything. The second-best kite maker on the street (Sami is the best, and he reminds her daily). She runs everywhere, climbs anything, and is bored by standing still. School girl in the preparatory years; top of her class in Arabic, bottom in patience.

**Want.** To win the kite contest and fly at Nour's wedding.

**Need.** To learn that holding on is its own kind of bravery. Teta asks her on Day 1 who is braver, the kite or the hand that holds the string; she says the kite, because "the hand just stands there". Over ten days the game takes away her running and gives her things to hold, and on Day 10 she answers: the hand.

**Her arc, mapped to what the player does.** Day 1 she runs (the kite run). Day 2 the shopping slows her down. Day 4 she carries water and cannot run. Day 5 she must hold completely still while the drone passes. Day 7 she carries, walks, and has to let go of her kite to raise her hands. Day 8 at the janazah she stands in the women's row and can do nothing; after it, only small tasks. Day 9 she holds the string while the whole street builds. Day 10 she holds. The mechanic is the arc.

**Voice.** Short, quick sentences; teasing; swears by "wallahi" when she is telling the truth and when she is not. Calls her grandmother Teta (or Sitti, if the readers choose), her father Baba, her brother by name or "ya Karim" when she wants something. Her notebook is her inner voice: lists, drawings, crossings-out, a child's honesty. Sample: "Tails don't win. Height wins." "It's only torn. I'll fix it tonight."

**How she moves.** In bursts. Runs, stops dead, spins to look back. Climbs with her whole body. When she is thinking she winds kite string around two fingers, a habit that must be in every idle animation until Day 7, missing on Day 8, and back on Day 10. When she is scared she goes very still and very straight.

**Silhouette and costume.** Small and quick. Outdoors and at school: a headscarf (colour per lock F-06, the readers confirm), as Gaza school custom expects of girls from about her age (the readers confirm the age), a long school dress or coat over trousers, trainers. At home among family: hair in a ponytail, house clothes. The change of silhouette between the street and the house is a small signal of safety, and it disappears after Day 7, when there is no house. Her readable trait is the kite and its string.

**Playable.** Every day. Physical verb: crawl through gaps, climb, fly the kite, run a fallen kite down. Social verb: children follow her.

**For the readers.** The headscarf age and colour for a Gaza schoolgirl; whether she says Teta or Sitti; how a 12-year-old Gazan girl teases a boy cousin without it reading as flirting.

### Teta Nawal, 71. Grandmother

**Who she is.** Baba's mother, a widow, the head of the house in everything except name. Born after the Nakba; her mother left their village in 1948 with the door key and the words "a week, two weeks". Teta keeps that key on a cord and tells her mother's stories as her own, which is how Palestinian memory works now that the Nakba generation is passing. She recites; she does not sing in front of guests. Dry, formidable, very funny. Every adult on the street opens their door to her.

**Want.** To see Nour married, and not to leave. **Fear.** A week that becomes a lifetime, again.

**Her thread.** Day 1 she asks the question. Day 2 she tells her mother's village on the roof and tries the key in the roof door (nothing opens, a joke the game repeats). Day 3 she whispers "hasbunallahu wa ni'mal wakeel" under the stairs before the bang. Day 3 she persuades Abu Ahmad to bake for the whole street. Day 7 she hangs up on the recorded evacuation call ("wrong number") and at the checkpoint raises her hands with the key closed in her fist. Day 9 she ties the bridle knot on Sami's rebuilt kite. Day 10 she tries the key on the school gate, laughs, and at the beach asks her question again.

**Voice.** Slow, certain, full of proverbs (the readers supply real Gazan proverbs; we do not invent them). Calls her grandchildren "ya sitti" or "ya teta" in the Levantine reversed address, if the readers confirm it. Sample: "Leave her, Rania. The wind doesn't wait for breakfast." "Heavy things are often tiny."

**How she moves.** Slowly and deliberately, cane in her right hand, a stoop. Stops on the stairs to breathe and pretends she stopped to look at something. Sits by putting one hand on the parapet first. Her hands are never idle: turning the key, picking over lentils, threading a needle.

**Silhouette and costume.** A white shasha headscarf with a long drape, a long embroidered thobe (tatreez on chest and sleeves), the cane, the stoop. The key's cord at her neck.

**Playable.** Day 3 (the bakery persuasion) and Day 9 (the knot), with others as the beat sheet says. Physical verb: cannot climb, walks slowly, carries only the bag. Social verb: every adult opens their door to her.

**For the readers.** Her mother's village (candidates the culture critique named for Gaza City families: Hamama, al-Majdal, Yibna, Isdud, Hiribya; the readers choose, or leave it unnamed); whether her biography rings true; her bedtime recitation (Ayat al-Kursi and the three Quls) as a real Gazan grandmother's practice; the line about Imam al-Shafi'i being born on this sand.

### Baba Yousef (Abu Karim), 44. Father

**Who he is.** A fisherman. The motor boats were burned, so he rows a paddle boat now, and went out by night in the good years. Quiet, strong, jokes with a straight face. Mends nets on the roof. Answers Karim's anger with silence, which makes it worse.

**Want.** To feed the family from the sea. **Fear.** The morning he can't.

**His thread.** Day 1 comes home at dawn with a small catch; at night he looks at the light on the horizon a second too long. Day 3 lifts the beam in the bakery. Day 6 rows out by day "so they can see we are only fishing", under the gunboat's searchlight, and brings the catch home to one table. Day 7 carries a neighbour on his back and says his name to the loudhailer with her weight on him. Day 8 his answer to Karim is not a lecture: he goes into their guns every morning for fish, and that is his. Day 9 gives a spar from his boat for Sami's kite.

**Voice.** Few words, dry. Sample: "Birds come home for breakfast." "Nine days of your mother's lists. Allah help us."

**How he moves.** A fisherman's rolling walk, arms a little away from the body, heavy on his heels. Hands never empty: a net, a rope, a basket, bread. Lifts with his legs, carefully, like a man whose back has been hurt once.

**Silhouette and costume.** A flat cap, broad shoulders, a net or rope over one shoulder, rolled trousers at the harbour.

**Playable.** Days 3, 6, 7 and wherever switching needs him. Physical verb: lift and carry heavy, push, row. Social verb: fishermen and the harbour.

### Mama Rania (Um Karim), 40. Mother

**Who she is.** A teacher at the neighbourhood UNRWA school. Organised, sharp, funny in a dry teacher's way, never still. When the school is shut she opens one in the stairwell with cardboard for slates. Her network is the mothers of the street and the clinic.

**Want.** For her children to keep learning, whatever happens. **Fear.** That Karim stops wanting anything.

**Her thread.** Day 1: the wedding lists ("flour" written four times). Day 2: the market. Day 5: the stairwell school and the first lesson, Iqra. Day 7: decides what goes in the bag. Day 8: wants Karim to put his name down for the scholarship, and says so, which is not the same as agreeing with Baba. Day 9: paper from her books for Sami's kite. Day 10: the women's hall.

**Voice.** Brisk, practical, lists. Sample: "Eat something before you fly away." "A wedding needs flour."

**How she moves.** Fast and efficient, bag of books on her shoulder, points with a pen, counts children with her finger.

**Silhouette and costume.** Hijab, long jilbab, the bag of books.

**Playable.** Days 2 and 5. Physical verb: read, repair, organise. Social verb: mothers and the clinic.

### Karim, 17. Brother

**Who he is.** A footballer, the best in the street. Angry, restless, clever with anything electronic: he charges, fixes and swaps phones for half the street. Wants to leave, wants to fight, wants to do something, and nobody will let him.

**Want.** To do something that matters. **Need.** To find what his own hands can do.

**His thread.** Day 1 asleep until the electricity comes on, then plugs three phones into one socket. Day 2 football at sunset (playable). Day 4 fetches water angrily, carries more than anyone. Day 6 wants to row out with Baba; Baba refuses. Day 7 at the checkpoint the loudhailer calls young men aside; he says his name clearly and is waved through after a long moment. Day 8 his choice (playable): put his name down for a scholarship abroad, or walk back up the road with the young men to dig out Abu Ahmad's oven; either way he is back by asr: he counts while Layla pulls the generator's cord, and at maghrib he fixes the courtyard speaker so the adhan is heard again. Day 9 string from his wire for Sami's kite; the men's sahja. Day 10 the zaffa and the dabke, played as Karim.

**Voice.** Sarcastic, short, calls Layla "ya zghireh" (little one) when she annoys him. Respectful with Baba, which is harder than shouting. Sample: "Why is everyone shouting? Oh. Power."

**How he moves.** Loose and bouncy, hands in pockets when angry, kicks stones, juggles a ball with his feet while he waits.

**Silhouette and costume.** Tall and thin, a track jacket, the ball.

**Playable.** Days 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10 and as switching needs. Physical verb: run fast, jump far, fix electronics. Social verb: young men of the street.

**For the readers.** What an angry 17-year-old in Gaza actually says, and what his family says back. Karim's choice must never frame leaving as salvation, and the answer to his anger must never be "violence is wrong".

## Next door: the uncle's house

The two houses share a wall and their roofs almost touch; Layla and Sami string the message line across that gap on Day 5.

### Sami, 12. Cousin

**Who he is.** Layla's cousin next door (ibn 'amm), son of Baba's younger brother, and her closest friend: the relation is for the readers to confirm. The best kite maker on the street; boastful and generous in the same breath; laughs at his own jokes before he finishes them. Always wants his kite to go out over the sea, to see what is on the other side.

**Want.** To win, and then to give it away. **His role.** He is the game's second child and its one death. The player must love him by Day 6. He is not Layla's redemption, not a lesson, and not a twist.

**His beats (from the design document).** Day 1: the contest and the promise, and his kite that falls into the street. Day 2: football, in goal, badly. Day 3: holds the candle in the bakery. Day 4: the jerrycan race, and the two queues split to double their chances. Day 5: the message line between the two roofs, and a note sent down it. Day 6: watches for Baba's boat from the beach with Layla, signalling with the kite. Day 7: dies off-screen in the strike behind the family as they walk; Layla learns it when someone says his name. Day 8: his janazah; his note, unread since Day 5, found in Layla's notebook. Day 9: the street rebuilds his kite, one part from each family. Day 10: Layla flies it.

**Voice.** Loud, happy, invents kite words. Calls Layla "ya bint 'ammi" (cousin) to annoy her. Sample: "That's not a tail, that's a wish." "Tails don't cheat. Tails hold on."

**How he moves.** Walks backwards while talking, spins his reel, big gestures, cannot stand still in a queue.

**Silhouette and costume.** Always carrying a kite or a reel; a cap.

**Playable.** Never.

**For the readers, before anything else in the game.** Everything about Day 7 and Day 8: how a Gazan child learns of a death, the janazah, the poster, the 'aza, whether names are written on children's arms, whether a martyr's funeral may rhyme with the wedding. The readers see these scenes first.

### Abu Sami, 42, and Um Sami, 40. Uncle and aunt

Baba's younger brother and his wife. Abu Sami is the quiet builder who taught Sami his knots and kites. On Day 10 he gives the blessing at his daughter's wedding two days after his son's janazah, if the founder accepts Decision 14 and the readers confirm the custom. Um Sami decides, on Day 8, that the wedding goes ahead: "He promised her a kite."

### Nour, 20. Sami's sister, the bride

Studying, laughing, tired. The wedding is hers. Seen only in women's spaces: the henna night on Day 9, the women's hall on Day 10 in a white dress with red tatreez. She is the one who says there is no wedding without a kite, and on Day 8 she is the one who says it again.

## The street: eight neighbour households

The design document asks for eight named neighbours, not twenty, whose needs change daily and who remember everything. These are the eight households. Each has one need the player can meet and one payoff the game gives back later without announcing it (the table is in `docs/story/beat-sheet.md`).

| Household | Who | Where on the street | Their thread |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Sami's family | Abu Sami, Um Sami, Nour, Sami | Next door; the roofs almost touch | The promise, the message line, the wedding |
| 2. The bakery | Abu Ahmad, 58, the baker | Mid-street, the warm door | Bread decides who eats. Day 3 bakes by candlelight on his hand-cranked machine; Day 8 his oven is dug out; Day 9 bread for the wedding |
| 3. The clinic | Um Samir, 35, the nurse, and her son Samir, 5 | The street's end | Bandages and generator fuel; Samir follows Layla everywhere; Day 8 the last battery |
| 4. The charging stall | Abu Fadi, 50, and his son Fadi, 24, the groom | The corner by the corniche | Light and news: six solar panels, phones at a shekel or two a charge; Day 9 the news of the kite announcement comes from his radio |
| 5. The twins | Hassan and Hussein, 9, and their mother Um Hassan | Across the street | Chalk race scores on the wall; bread on Day 3, water race on Day 4, paper for Sami's kite on Day 9 |
| 6. The old fisherman | Abu Khalil, 70s, alone | A ground-floor room by the steps to the sea | Marbles with Layla; his boat burned long ago; from Day 3 he calls the adhan unaided from his roof, and on Day 8 his voice goes through Karim's repaired speaker |
| 7. Teta's friend | Hajja Amina, 80s | Across the street, laundry on her roof | Cannot climb; her door is always open to Teta; Day 1 laundry, Day 4 water, Day 7 carried (or not), Day 10 saves Layla a seat |
| 8. The mosque | The imam, Sheikh Ibrahim (working name), 50s | The mosque at the top of the street, its minaret over every roof | Day 3 the minaret is hit; Day 8 he leads the janazah; Day 10 he conducts the nikah and says Quran 30:21 |

**How they move and read in silhouette.**

- Abu Ahmad: broad, floury apron, wipes his hands, laughs from the belly, carries trays on his head.
- Um Samir: hijab and white coat, quick and purposeful, a bag of dressings; Samir at her hem.
- Abu Fadi: cables around his neck, a phone in each hand, talks with both.
- Fadi: shy, tall, helps his father; a groom's jacket and kuffiyeh on Day 10.
- The twins: identical, always in step, bumping into each other, two kites.
- Abu Khalil: sea legs, sits on an upturned crate, flicks marbles with his thumb, a kuffiyeh round his neck.
- Hajja Amina: tiny, bent, a big white scarf, always leaning out of a window.
- Sheikh Ibrahim: a long jubba and a white cap, measured steps, hands behind his back.

**For the readers.** Every name; whether a neighbourhood imam as a character is right, and how he should be shown; the twins' names; Abu Khalil's adhan from a roof after the minaret is hit, which the culture critique called documented but no verification file carries.

## Mishmish, the street cat

Orange, thin, shameless. Follows whoever carries fish. Adopts the family on Day 1, hides under the stairs with them on Day 3, steals a shrimp on Day 6, follows the column on Day 7 because Baba has bread in his pocket, and is on the beach on Day 10. Mishmish survives everything. A joy beat on the worst days, and it costs almost nothing.

## "They"

Not a character, never a target, never a face. Present as things and sounds: the zanana (the drone's hum) from Day 2; a light on the horizon on Day 1; the gunboat with its searchlight, loudhailer and water cannon on Day 6; leaflets, an SMS and a recorded call on Day 7; a tank's shape at the end of the coastal road and soldiers as shadows behind lights on a berm at the checkpoint on Day 7, with a loudhailer that says "hands up, phone up, say your name"; the announcement on Day 9 that a kite is a drone. The leaflet and the loudhailers speak Arabic; the culture critique proposed this and the readers confirm the wording, which must follow the documented leaflet text ("For your safety, you need to evacuate your places of residence immediately and head to known shelters") and the documented checkpoint order ("raise your hands and phones and state your names").
