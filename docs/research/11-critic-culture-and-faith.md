# critic_v2_9a2c0f31654

## Verdict
Caveat first: I am a stand-in, not a Palestinian sensitivity reader. Nothing below replaces two paid Gazan readers with binding notes, and several items (Sitti vs Teta, which family name, the funeral chant, the martyr's-wedding custom, whether kite duels exist in Gaza) must be settled by them, not by me. With that said: the spine of the doc is sound and worth protecting (no combat, joy as a pillar, real places and food, violence heard not shown, hope as staying). But as written the doc fails the founder's own halal requirement outright (the entire audio plan is instruments), contains no religious life at all for a Gazan family (no prayer, adhan, mosque, dua, Jumu'ah, janazah), and carries a handful of details that a Palestinian reader would catch in the first pass as imported from a Western or South Asian frame (the surname Haddad, a 71-year-old who "remembers" 1948, a boat escape from Gaza, glass-string kite duels, Dr Israr Ahmed lectures in a Gaza home, "marrying off a cousin", "name on the wall"). One structural ethical risk must be removed before any prototype: the doc lets player choices decide which names end up on the wall, which makes a Gazan family responsible for who dies. All of this is fixable in a v0.2 without touching the engine, the ten-day structure or the cast list, and most fixes make the game more specific and therefore better.

## Top issues
- [blocker] The Audio section is entirely haram under the founder's stated rule. It specifies oud, qanun, ney, riq, darbuka, piano, strings and synth pads, plus arranged folk songs and a 'licensed-feeling' Life is Strange score.
  why: The mainstream Sunni position (all four madhhabs' majority view) is that melodic instruments are impermissible; the one instrument with explicit textual permission is the duff (plain frame drum) at weddings and Eid (the hadith 'the difference between the halal and the haram is the duff and the voice at weddings'). Ney is a flute (explicitly named in the prohibitions), darbuka is not a duff, riq has jingles (contested), and synthesised melodic pads are treated by most scholars as instruments because they imitate them. The founder wrote 'absolutely halal: NO haram instruments'; the doc as written would be rejected by him on first read and by the audience on first trailer.
  fix: Rewrite the score as voice-only: layered male nasheed (melody, harmony drone, breath, hummed lines, vocal percussion, clapping), with the duff (plain frame, no jingles) used only in the wedding scenes (zaffa, sahja, dabke) where it belongs. Keep the adaptive-layer idea: the day/night/threat/rest layers become vocal drones, wind, sea, and the drone hum. The 'one main theme in Hijaz' survives as a hummed nasheed melody; you can still compose it in code as MIDI, but render it with human voices (the friend singer plus anyone else you can record), never with a soundfont oud. Replace 'Dal'ona, Zareef al-Tool, Wein a Ramallah arranged' with men's a cappella sahja chants at the wedding, which is how conservative Gazan weddings actually sound. Delete the 'Music is the emotional engine of Life is Strange' framing; the emotional engine here is the human voice, the adhan and the sea.

- [blocker] Prayer, the adhan, the mosque, dua and Jumu'ah are absent from the game. The five places are bakery, clinic, UN school, harbour and beach; 'prayer calls' appear once as CC0 ambience.
  why: In any Gaza street the adhan is the loudest structural sound of the day and the mosque is as central as the bakery. A devout family's day is shaped by five prayers; a game about 'one day at a time' with phases named Morning, Noon, Dusk, Night and no salah would be read by every Palestinian as written by someone who has never been in a Muslim home. The founder's audience will also expect it.
  fix: Make the day phases the prayers: Fajr, Dhuhr, Asr, Maghrib, Isha, with the adhan from the minaret as the clock that turns the phase. Add the mosque to the five places. Any ten consecutive days contain a Friday: put Jumu'ah in the siege act and hold it on the rubble of the mosque (documented in 2023-24); use the khutbah as the scene where 'the street organises'. Day 3: the minaret is hit; Abu Ahmad calls the adhan from his roof unaided (documented). Day 8's joy beat becomes 'Karim repairs the mosque loudspeaker; the adhan returns to the street' instead of 'music'. The rooftop moment is Maghrib over the sea. Show prayer in silhouette (rows, ruku, sujud) which is beautiful in this art style and costs nothing. Sit spots include a prayer mat. Fill dialogue with the actual grammar of Gazan speech: bismillah, alhamdulillah, inshallah, ya rabb, Allah yustur, Allah yehmeena, hasbunallahu wa ni'mal wakeel (the phrase people say under bombardment), Allah kareem, Allah yerhamo; leave several untranslated in the English subtitles.

- [blocker] Quran usage has no etiquette rules and the founder's specific asks (famous reciters' clips, Dr Israr Ahmed lectures, Quran 'instead of music') need constraints and rights.
  why: Quran is not ambience. Quran 7:204 commands attentive silence when it is recited; playing it under jumping, puzzles or on a loop in a menu, cutting it mid-ayah when the player walks away, or mixing it with a duff or a rumble bus would offend the very audience the founder wants. Famous reciters' recordings are copyrighted performances even though the text is not, and a commercial Steam game needs written permission. Dr Israr Ahmed is a Pakistani scholar who lectured in Urdu; a Gazan family would never have his lectures on; putting them in Gaza is the founder's own (Indian subcontinent) reference frame leaking into Palestine and is the single most 'outsider' note in the feedback. AI-generated recitation (ElevenLabs cloning a reciter) has been condemned by scholarly bodies and would be a scandal.
  fix: Rules: Quran only in stillness (sit spots, cutscenes, the bakery radio at Fajr, Teta reciting Yasin at night); never under player traversal; never on a loop; never mixed with any music layer, duff or rumble; if the player moves, fade only at an ayah boundary or lock input for one or two short ayat; Quranic text on screen in a proper Uthmani font (KFGQPC Uthmanic HAFS or Amiri Quran), exempt from the grain, ink-bleed and wobble shaders, with a licensed English translation. Never AI recitation. Either obtain written permission from a reciter's estate or label, or record a hafiz friend and make him a character: the street's hafiz, a real archetype in every Gaza neighbourhood. Keep the reference frame Gazan: Abdul Basit, Minshawi and al-Husary are the Egyptian voices a Gaza household grew up with, and Palestinian reciters exist. Honour the founder's Dr Israr wish in the thanks or a separate reflections page, not inside the fiction. Let Quran and hadith appear in the characters' mouths at the moment they would say them (Inna lillahi on hearing of a death, Ayat al-Kursi before sleep, the salawat at the zaffa), not as chapter-card epigraphs, which is exactly the pamphlet the doc says it wants to avoid.

- [blocker] Player choices decide who dies. 'Which names are on the wall' is listed as an outcome of the Day 7 carry choice and the generator-battery choice.
  why: This is the This War of Mine logic, and in Gaza it is wrong: it makes a Palestinian family's decision the cause of a death that 'they' caused. Palestinians are, for good reason, acutely sensitive to being framed as responsible for their own suffering. It also contradicts the doc's own pillar that a child's death is never a twist or a punishment.
  fix: Make every death fixed, unavoidable and clearly caused by the occupation. Choices change presence and light only: who is at the wedding table, who was evacuated where, which windows are lit, whether the clinic or the school has power tonight, what Teta recites, whether the kite is Layla's or Sami's family's. Reword pillar 5 to say this explicitly. Reword 'Children are protected in the fiction' to: 'The child protagonist is never harmed on screen, and no child's death is ever a twist, a surprise reveal, or a consequence of a player choice.'

- [major] The era is undefined and the doc mixes two Gazas. Kite festivals, wedding shopping, football at sunset, a working harbour, UNRWA schools as schools and a 'siege' with one off-screen death are blockade-era (2007-2023) textures; 'the real, ongoing catastrophe with real dead' is 2023-26, where none of Day 1 or Day 2 could happen and the scale of death is not one.
  why: A Gazan reader in 2026 will ask which Gaza this is. Set against the present, a story with a festival, a wedding at the end and one death reads as sanitising. Set in the blockade era with a fictional escalation (the rhythm of 2012, 2014 or 2021), it is honest and the joy pillar is plausible.
  fix: State the decision in the doc and in the sources tab: this is a fictional escalation during the blockade, and the game is dedicated to what came after. Keep 'the year is never stated' in the fiction but not in the paratext. Drop 'Not a game about a war' from the press angle; Palestinians will say it is one. Keep 'a street that refuses to stop being a street' without denying the war.

- [major] The family name Haddad reads as Palestinian Christian.
  why: Haddad (blacksmith) is one of the most common Palestinian Christian surnames. Muslim Haddads exist, but for a devout Sunni Gazan family the name makes readers pause on page one, and in Gaza a surname identifies a real hamula (clan).
  fix: Choose a Gazan Muslim family name tied to a 1948 village of origin, since most Gaza City-area families are refugees from Hamama, al-Majdal, Yibna, Isdud, Hiribya and similar. Candidates a reader should vet against real prominent families: Abu Amra, Hamdan, Ashour, Abu Shaaban, al-Masri (very common in Gaza). Avoid Bakr: it is the real fishing family of Shati camp whose four boys were killed on the beach in 2014, so a fisherman family named Bakr on that beach is either a tribute needing consent or an appropriation. Locate the street: Shati camp or al-Mina, the actual fishing neighbourhoods. Have neighbours address the parents by kunya (Abu Karim, Umm Karim), since adults are not first-named in the street. Let readers decide Sitti versus Teta; Sitti is the more Gazan word.

- [major] Teta is 71 and 'keeps the 1948 house key' with stories of the village. If the game is set today she was born around 1955, seven years after the Nakba.
  why: She cannot remember the village. A Palestinian reader would flag it instantly, and the key is already the most reproduced symbol in Palestinian iconography, so getting the biography wrong makes it a prop.
  fix: Either make her 84 or older (born 1940, eight at the Nakba, remembers the sea at al-Majdal), or, truer to 2026, keep 71 and make the key her mother's: the stories she tells are her mother's stories, which is how Palestinian memory now actually works as the Nakba generation passes. Her line for Day 1 becomes stronger for it.

- [major] Women's silhouettes are Western defaults: Layla's readable trait is a 'ponytail', Rania and Um Samir have no head covering.
  why: In Gaza virtually all women wear hijab outside the home and most girls begin around Layla's age. A 12-year-old with a loose ponytail in the street and a hijabless teacher would read as false to Palestinians and as non-halal to the founder's audience.
  fix: Layla: a scarf whose tail streams in the wind, which visually rhymes with the kite string and lets her silhouette read the wind field; the loose-scarf-plus-school-uniform look of a Gazan girl. Rania: hijab, long jilbab, bag of books. Um Samir: hijab and nurse's coat. Teta: white headscarf (shasha) and embroidered thobe, the classic grandmother outline. Inside the home hub with only family present you may leave them uncovered; the 'cover, Abu Ahmad is at the door' scramble is a real household joke and a free joy beat, but the safe default in silhouette is to keep them covered.

- [major] The wedding is drawn as a Western wedding: 'the dress', a dabke, 'marrying off a cousin', an unnamed couple, and no separation of men and women.
  why: Gaza weddings are gender-segregated. The bride is seen only by women; the men have the sahra with sahja chanting and dabke; the groom arrives in a zaffa with duff and salawat. 'Marrying off a cousin' is orientalist phrasing that implies coercion. Mixed dabke or the couple framed together romantically would break the halal rule and the truth.
  fix: Name the couple and their relation to the family (for example Sami's older sister, if Sami becomes a cousin). Day 9's joy beat becomes two halves: the henna night (women's side, Layla's POV: the tatreez thobe, zaghareet, women's a cappella songs) and the men's sahja and dabke practice (Karim's POV, duff and voices only). Day 10: the zaffa brings the groom to the door; the men's courtyard and the women's hall are separate scenes; the player sees the whole wedding only by switching characters, which is a mechanic nobody in the fiction has and turns segregation into design rather than an apology. No couple shots, no 'first look'. Add the real conversation that would happen: whether to hold a wedding days after a martyrdom in the street; Gazans did in the shelters, and the martyr's father giving his blessing is a scene worth the whole game.

- [major] Layla (girl, 12) and Sami (boy, 12) are 'best friends' who share 'secrets'.
  why: By 12, Gazan boys and girls are largely in separate schools and social worlds; a Western player will read the pair as a budding romance and a conservative player as improper. It also sets up the Kite Runner arc (Amir and Hassan) the founder said to avoid.
  fix: Make Sami her cousin next door (ibn 'amm), which is how a Gazan girl would have a boy as a daily companion, or make the character Samia. If Sami stays a boy and dies, do not let his death become Layla's guilt engine; her story is care, not redemption.

- [major] The death of a child is written in Western memorial imagery: 'an empty chair and a name on the wall'.
  why: The name-on-the-wall is a Vietnam-memorial trope. Gazans have their own truthful, dignified images, and the founder has said the death can be shown.
  fix: Show what Gazans do. The strike is heard; Layla learns from the street's sound (women's cries, men shouting Allahu akbar at the site). The small body in a white shroud carried on the father's arms; the name written on the child's arm in marker so he can be identified (the 2023 practice). Salat al-janazah in the courtyard in silhouette, four takbirs, Layla in the women's row at the back. Same-day burial off screen. The 'aza tent for three days with bitter coffee, men and women separate, neighbours arriving; Karim hand-draws the martyr's poster for the wall because there is no print shop. The family's words: 'istashhad' (he was martyred), never 'passed away' or 'we lost him'; 'Alhamdulillah, he is with Allah, Allah chose him', then real tears. Use the Prophetic model for the scene: the eye weeps and the heart grieves and the tongue says only what pleases Allah (Bukhari, on the death of his son Ibrahim). Never: the moment of impact, blood, slow motion, an 'angry at God' beat, 'he is in a better place'. Never a twist. His kite stays in the world.

- [major] How the perpetrator is named is undecided; the founder wants honesty without cowardice; the doc's 'enemy has no face' is justified by marketing ('impossible to dismiss as a pamphlet').
  why: Palestinian readers will ask who the vagueness protects. At the same time, dialogue that names Israel in every line becomes a slogan, and one real Gazan usage ('al-yahud', which many people say colloquially) is a landmine in translation and must not be in the game.
  fix: Speak like Gazans in the fiction: 'humma' (they), 'al-ihtilal' (the occupation), 'al-zanana' (the drone), the gunboat, the F-16, the tank, the fence, the checkpoint, the leaflets. Let the perpetrator name itself through its own artefacts, which is documentary rather than slogan: the Arabic evacuation leaflet on Day 7 signed by the army, the recorded phone call ordering evacuation, the navy loudspeaker addressing fishermen in Arabic on Day 6. Name Israel plainly in the paratext: sources tab, store page, credits, with the blockade since 2007 and Egypt's role. Rewrite pillar 2's justification: 'Gazans never see a face. They see drones, gunboats, tanks, leaflets and a searchlight. We show exactly what they see.' Ban protest slogans as dialogue; the only chants in the game are the ones that belong to a scene, such as the funeral chant, and those are approved by readers.

- [major] Soldiers and gunboats in silhouette: the founder wants them shown 'in some way'; the doc borrows 'wordless dread' from Inside, whose dread comes from being chased by men.
  why: Silhouetted machines and distant figures are true to what Gaza City residents actually see and keep the camera on the people. Sneaking past soldiers as puzzle obstacles would turn the occupation into a stealth mechanic and invite the 'Inside with Palestinians' read.
  fix: Yes to: the gunboat as a horizon shape with a sweeping searchlight, water cannon and loudspeaker on Day 6; tanks and a line of helmets on a distant ridge on Day 7; a quadcopter voice; leaflets falling. No to: soldier encounters as chase or stealth puzzles, any negotiation or deference scene, any soldier as a character. The family's dignity is the scene: Baba keeps his net, Teta keeps her key.

- [major] The founder's line 'we don't be cowards, we also go straight in and fight them' has no answer in the doc.
  why: He is telling you the design feels soft. The honest answer is that sumud is itself resistance ('our existence is resistance' is a Palestinian phrase), but the game must show that rather than assert it, and it must not answer Karim's anger with a Western pacifist line.
  fix: Make defiance mechanical without a weapon: on Day 6 the player as Baba chooses to cross the three-mile line under the water cannon and keeps fishing; consequences are the boat damaged and nets confiscated, as documented, not death. Teta's refusal to leave. Karim says 'let me go and fight them' and the family's answer is the real one: his mother tells him staying alive and studying in the stairwell is his front, his father says he goes into their guns every morning for fish and that is his. The wedding held anyway. The funeral procession's chant, if readers approve it, is grief and defiance in one breath. The one thing the answer must not be is 'violence is wrong'.

- [major] Karim's choice is framed as 'leaves on a boat or stays'.
  why: Nobody leaves Gaza by boat; the sea is sealed and boats are intercepted within miles. It is a Mediterranean-migrant image, not a Gazan one. Leaving Gaza means Rafah, a coordination fee of thousands of dollars per person, or a scholarship abroad.
  fix: Karim's choice becomes: register for a scholarship or a paid Rafah 'coordination' the family cannot afford, versus staying; or volunteering with civil defence and the ambulances, which is what angry 17-year-olds in Gaza actually do. Never frame leaving for the West as the real salvation (the Kite Runner ending).

- [major] 'Kite duels with glass-coated string are a real Gaza pastime' is asserted, not sourced.
  why: Glass-coated cutting string is manja (India, Pakistan) and tar (Afghanistan, and the Kite Runner's signature). I cannot confirm it as a Gaza tradition. The documented Gaza kite culture is mass beach flying (the 2011 UNRWA record at the beach) and homemade kites from plastic bags and sticks. This reads as the founder's South Asian frame and the Kite Runner both leaking in. Separately, 'kites from Gaza' carries the 2018 incendiary-kite framing in Israeli media, so the game's kites must be unmistakably children's kites.
  fix: Verify with Gazan readers. If unconfirmed, replace the duel with highest, longest, most kites in the air, or a kite race along the beach, and keep the 'kite carries messages' mechanic. Cite the 2011 record in the sources tab.

- [major] The Kite Runner is named as a liked reference with no list of what to avoid.
  why: The book is criticised in Afghanistan for explaining Afghans to Americans: the loyal poorer friend (Hassan) who exists to die for the protagonist's guilt; redemption as the survivor's arc; escape to the West as salvation; trauma (a child's assault) as plot engine; kite fighting exoticised as colourful native custom; the Taliban as cartoon villain.
  fix: Write the avoid-list into the doc: no dead friend as the protagonist's redemption; no guilt-then-atonement arc for Layla; no 'leave for the West' salvation for Karim; no sexual violence or hidden shame as engine; kites are ordinary children's play, not an exotic set piece; the audience is not the West needing Gaza explained. Borrow only the two things worth borrowing: a child's eye on a city about to change, and the kite as a line between sky and hand.

- [major] 'Why this will sell' and parts of the feeling statement read as monetising solidarity and as the 'resilient Palestinian' trope.
  why: 'None of them have been about Gaza' as a market gap, 'the audience is already mobilised', and 'not victims but the strongest people they have ever met' will land badly with Palestinian readers, many of whom reject resilience framing ('we do not want to be resilient, we want to be free'). Aiming for a stranger to 'feel something' and close the game is the definition of trauma tourism if nothing follows.
  fix: Move the commercial reasoning to a separate internal doc. In the GDD, state instead: Palestinians are paid (readers, singer, consultants, credited by name if they wish), the charity share is fixed and audited, and the sources tab is a way for players to act. Replace the feeling statement: the family is allowed to be tired, afraid and angry; 'they are still there' is not a comfort for the player but a fact the player has to sit with. Keep the joy pillar; it is the correct antidote.

- [minor] Small texture errors a Gazan would catch: musakhan as the signature dish; 'gossip' listed as a joy; 'a game of cards' as a neighbour need; bakery 'by candlelight'; 'prayer calls' as CC0 background noise; football on the beach at sunset.
  why: Musakhan is northern West Bank; Gaza has its own cuisine. Gheebah (backbiting) is a major sin and a devout founder will notice 'gossip' as a joy beat. Backgammon is explicitly prohibited in a hadith and cards are contested, so neither fits a halal game. Gaza bakeries run wood-fired taboons with 4am queues, not candles. Football at sunset on the Gaza beach evokes the four Bakr boys killed there in 2014, which should be a conscious choice, not an accident.
  fix: Food: keep sumaqiyya, add rummaniyya, dagga (Gaza's hot pepper and dill salad), zibdiyit gambari, sayadieh (fish rice, perfect for a fisherman family), and Gazan knafeh (semolina and nuts, no cheese), plus shatta on everything; Gazans eat spicy and are proud of it. Joy beats: 'news and teasing', not 'gossip'. Games: football, marbles, seven stones, chess (least contested); no cards, no backgammon, no argileh or cigarettes for the family. Bakery: the taboon glow and the dawn queue. Football: either honour the Bakr boys in the sources tab or move the match to the school yard.

- [minor] Founder proposes Google's generative music tool and ElevenLabs for voices.
  why: A generative music model will produce instruments, which are out. Shipped AI voices in a Gaza game will be read by the community as 'they would not even give us real voices', and an AI voice reciting Quran would be a scandal.
  fix: Use both only as scratch placeholders in this week's prototype, clearly labelled, never in a public build. Ship the friend's voice, the founder's own community, and a recorded hafiz. State this in the doc so it survives the timeline pressure.

## Missed opportunities
- Make the ten days the first ten days of Dhul Hijjah, ending on Eid al-Adha in the rubble (documented in 2024): the wedding falls on Eid, the whole street chants the takbirat al-Eid (voice only, halal, and the most recognisable sound a Muslim audience knows), and 'ten days' carries meaning for the founder's audience for free.
- Layla writes 'Bismillah ar-Rahman ar-Rahim' at the top of every notebook page, as every Gazan schoolchild does; the notebook becomes recognisably hers and the save summary becomes an act of faith.
- Teta's lullaby becomes what Palestinian grandmothers actually do at bedtime: reciting Ayat al-Kursi and the three Quls and blowing over the child. It is halal, true, and more moving than a song.
- The zanana (drone hum) as the constant sound of the siege act. It is the single most recognisable sound of Gaza and it is absent from the doc; it also gives the rumble bus something to do between strikes.
- Imam al-Shafi'i was born in Gaza (767 CE). One line from Teta on the roof ('the Imam was born on this sand') gives the family's faith a Gazan root rather than a generic one.
- Character switching as the only way to see a segregated wedding whole: Layla sees the henna night, Karim the men's sahja, and only the player sees both. This is a design idea no Western game could have.
- The martyr's funeral is called his 'wedding' ('urs al-shahid) in Palestinian custom and the mother ululates. A game with a wedding on Day 10 and a funeral in the middle could rhyme them deliberately. It is easy to get wrong and Western critics will misread it, so it belongs with the readers, but it is the most Palestinian idea available to this story.
- Concrete routes to paid Gazan readers, since the founder has none: We Are Not Numbers (a Gaza youth writing project) as a source of writers to pay as readers; Rasheed Abueideh, the Palestinian developer of Liyla and the Shadows of War and Dreams on a Pillow, as a peer to approach with respect rather than as 'prior art to study'.
- A 'Gazan, not South Asian' checklist in the doc for the founder's own use: Sitti not Dadi, Egyptian reciters not Pakistani lecturers, taboon not tandoor, sahja not qawwali, Palestinian nasheed not Urdu nasheed, kunyas not first names, and no manja.
- Kite campaign moderation: dedications to real killed children should be possible only through families or organisations that consent, with free-text moderated, so the ending's sky is a tribute and not a scraped list.
- Answer the founder's colour question honestly: yes, more colour, because a grey Gaza is the news's Gaza. Bougainvillea, blue fishing boats, tatreez red, the sea and the market are the family's Gaza; let siege days steal the colour rather than starting without it.
- Check the title against the 2025 Global Sumud Flotilla for Steam search and press collision before the store page goes live.

## Keep
- No combat, and steadfastness as verbs: carry, repair, share, teach, cook, wait, fly. This is the correct spine and it is what Palestinians mean by sumud.
- The joy pillar and the rule that a day that is only sad gets redesigned. Gazan humour is real and it is the best defence against trauma tourism.
- Specificity over genericising: Gaza named, real places, real food, Gazan dialect, and the refusal to move the story to an unnamed country.
- Violence heard, felt and remembered, never shown; failure as a white screen of dust with no death animation.
- No slogans in dialogue. Keep this rule exactly as written; the fixes above give the honesty another route.
- The sources tab with each day's events traced to testimony, and the commitment that readers' notes are binding.
- Hope as staying, not rescue, and the ending on the sea with the kites.
- The Day 7 carry choice made with the hands rather than a menu, provided it never decides who dies.
- Tatreez as the interface language; it is grid-based, real, and Palestinian rather than decoratively 'Arab'.
- Kites as the only pure colour, the wind as a visible field, and the wedding in the school courtyard among the rubble, which is documented and true.
- The documented textures already in the doc: rolling blackouts, water trucks, jerrycan races, stairwell schools, rooftop solar, the fishing limit, the UN school as shelter.
- Arabic on screen with English below so the dialect is heard in the player's head; Godot's native RTL shaping and Amiri and Noto Naskh under OFL.
- Accessibility as launch requirements, especially the separate rumble bus and the photosensitivity mode.
- The fixed, audited charity share stated on the store page.
- Um Samir the nurse and Rania the teacher: Gazan women are highly educated and hold these roles; the cast gets this right.
